Workmanship warranty versus material and product coverage

The source guide uses one precise phrase: ask what warranty applies to workmanship and what is excluded. That wording separates two different layers of protection, and confusing them is where homeowners get caught.

  • Workmanship warranty covers the quality of the installation itself: cabinetry hung level, drawers aligned, plumbing and electrical work completed correctly, seals and joins finished properly.
  • Material or product coverage attaches to items such as hardware, benchtops or appliances, and normally comes from the manufacturer rather than the builder.
  • Exclusions are whatever the written terms leave out, and the source guide treats reading them as a non-negotiable step before signing.

The guide also mentions Blum certified installers for soft-close drawers and hinges, walk-in pantry designs, pull-out systems and corner carousels. Features like these are daily-use items, which makes the workmanship layer especially relevant: a soft-close mechanism that fails because it was fitted badly is an installation question, not a product question. Homeowners should ask which layer each promised feature falls under before comparing quotes.

Why licensing decides whether a warranty is real

The source states that Queensland building work over $3,300 requires a current QBCC licence, and that licensed plumbers and electricians are required by Queensland law. This is the load-bearing fact behind every warranty conversation.

A workmanship promise only has practical weight when the person making it is licensed for the work being done. If a contractor above the stated threshold cannot show a current QBCC licence, or if plumbing and electrical tasks are not allocated to licensed trades, the homeowner's position weakens no matter what the paperwork says. Industry bodies such as the Housing Industry Association and Master Builders Australia consistently frame licence verification as a first step when engaging a builder, and consumer guidance from ASIC Moneysmart's renovating pages similarly puts checking credentials before paying deposits.

The quoting habit the source recommends is simple: confirm licence status early, in writing, alongside the warranty terms. If a quote is silent on both, treat that as information about the quote.

Writing the warranty question into the written quote

The source guide warns that a lower number is not necessarily better if it leaves important work outside the written scope. The same test applies to warranty terms. A quote can look competitive while excluding removal, plumbing, electrical work, splashbacks, lighting or appliance integration, and each exclusion is also a gap in what any workmanship promise sensibly covers.

  1. Ask for the workmanship warranty term in writing, with the exclusions listed rather than implied.
  2. Match the warranty scope to the quoted scope: if lighting or plumbing is in the quote, the workmanship promise should say so explicitly.
  3. Name the products and hardware in the quote, since the source mentions soft-close hardware, pull-out systems and pantry fittings that carry their own product coverage.
  4. Record the licence details of the builder and the licensed trades alongside the warranty clause, so the two travel together.

None of this requires legal drafting. It requires the questions to be asked before signing rather than after a fault appears.

  2. Ask the workmanship question in writing. Put the source guide's question verbatim: what warranty applies to workmanship, and what is excluded.
  3. Match warranty scope to quoted scope. Cross-check exclusions such as removal, plumbing, electrical, lighting and appliance integration against the written quote.
  4. Record products and hardware. Name cabinetry hardware, benchtop category and lighting in the quote so product coverage and workmanship coverage do not blur.
  5. Compare terms, not just totals. A cheaper quote with wider warranty exclusions can cost more over the life of the kitchen than a higher, clearly written one.
